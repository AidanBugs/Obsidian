# Reading Response Paper 1: Dunne's "Theory, Techne and Phronesis"
This chapter of Dunne's book is a critique on Aristotle's notions of phronesis and techne and Dunne attempts to point out the flaws in separating techne from the realm of theory and that phronesis is much more distinguished from theory than techne is. Thus Dunne argues that despite Aristotle's efforts to to categorize techne as a form of productive knowledge separate to theory, techne still adheres closely to the realm of theory unlike its practical counterpart phronesis which Aristotle spent less time fleshing out its ideals.

Dunne does a good job of building the foundation of his arguement by first familiarizing the reader with the Platonic notions of episteme and the other various forms of theoretical knowledge in Aristotle's works. Following this section, Dunne then explains Aristotle's perspective on how he believes episteme remains above techne, that theory is above the practical. Within this section of the chapter, Dunne also explores the flaws and irony of this way of thinking and that theory without the practical is infact meaningless. Dunne also points out that between techne and phronesis, Aristotle hardly attempts to give any sort of systemic structure to phronesis like its supposed untechnical counterpart techne. 

Dunne then argues that Aristotle doesn't quite understand the differences and nuances between techne and phronesis because of fact that he has overall been super dismissive towards phronesis. It is page 247 where Dunne points out that it seems that Aristotle only really differentiates them by the fact that techne is productive in the sense it is usually more physical, while phronesis is much more practical in the sense it concerns itself with only actions. This then is analagous to poieseis and praxeis, which are two distinct types of activities. Praxeis is activities which end with themselves, while poieseis refers to activities whose end is beyond the activity. Using these terms, it is clear that techne is akin to poieseis while phronesis is praxeis.

Using these analogies, Dunne dives deeper into the definition of techne. It is here that Dunne looks at the distinction between what Aristotle says about techne and phronesis versus what how he actually uses these terms. Dunne then explores Aristotle's definition of techne and argues that techne is linked to episteme through its nature of being a well structured productive knowledge. As such, Dunne believes Aristotle holds techne above phronesis because techne comes from knowledge and understanding while phronesis is simply learned from memorizing experiences. Aristotle hold techne higher because he defines it as a more intellectual matter as opposed to phronesis which he believes is largely learned through memorizing coincedental experiences. 

After Dunne further analyzes Aristotle's dismissive behaviour towards phronesis, he is able to draw a quite clear distinction between phronesis and techne because phronesis is only concerned with the excellence of an individual and thus cannot be theorized into something taught to others as phronesis is excellence for one's own actions. This is a huge distinction from techne which was previously explained to be capable of being taught do to its structured nature, whereas phronesis cannot be taught do to its individualistic nature. 

Now that Dunne has fully fleshed out both phronesis and techne, he further analyzes the issues with how Aristotle uses and percieves these terms. For starters, Dunne argues it is unclear whether or not Aristotle himself actually approves of using these terms as he is very clear that he disapproves in any sort of simplistic use of the terms arguing that phronesis is not an excellence that one is able to do but rather an excellence in something they must do. Additionally, Dunne argues that Aristotle isn't very systematic in his use of means and ends for techne and phronesis despite their very crucial role in distinguishing the differences between the two.

Thus the overall structure of the text is first preparing the user by introducing the vocabulary of Aristotle's works then analyzes and critiques them by quoting Aristotle, explaining potential interpretations, then pointing out the flaws within these frames of thinking and how Aristotle uses these terms.
