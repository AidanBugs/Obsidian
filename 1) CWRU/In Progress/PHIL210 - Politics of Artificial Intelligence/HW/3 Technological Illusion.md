# Reading Respponse Paper 3
Leroi-Gourhan opens the text talking about how we as a society often speak on the fear of techniques and technology over taking us. Rather than agree with this perspective, Leroi-Gourhan questions as to if this is really a new problem or if instead we are prisoners to the illusion that technology will over take us. As such, Leroi-Gourhan argues that technique is far from overtaking us and in fact, this fear has been an integral part of our history and is what has driven us to develop as humans. 

The first proponent of his argument is that since ancient times technique is what makes us humans. Leroi-Gourhan emphasized that the anatomical structure of our skeleton is not enough to separate homo sapiens from Australopithecenes or Pithecanthropians, rather the presence of stone tools is what made the Pithecanthropians humans. Leroi-Gourhan then argues that while an anatomist could argue that a fossilized skull could not have possibly contained a brain capable of human thought and ingenuity, if the skull is found with a tool then that skull must belong to a human. Thus, this demonstrates that the tool is a better indicator of humanity than the skull itself, which he referred to as technical overtaking.

Leroi-Gourhan further evolves notion of technical overtaking arguing that technique, through the millions of interactions of tools and workers, improve cummulatively in very small incriments, similar to evolution in species. Additionally, since these advancements are a cummulative product of human thought, techniques are ahead of any single individual that uses it. Additionally, despite the ever so vast advancements in techniques the human technical brain has not advanced significantly in the past 40,000 years, arguing that our physical precision is not better than they were at the beginning. 

However despite the fact the technical overtaking that is present (and has been present), Leroi-Gourhan argues that this is actually not a danger. This is because techniques have always evolved externally to the human body, which ultimately leaves us "free at the physiological level". The true catastrophe would have been if technical perfectibility had been integrated into our brain's biology. Leroi-Gourhan argues that if humans were integrated with an everso complicated technical brain then techniques would become hereditary and we would become like ants. However, like mentioned above, the human technical brain has not advanced significantly and our gestures are no more precise then when we were neanderthals, Leroi-Gourhan argues that we should not concern ourselves with the fate of the human ant. 

Instead, Leroi-Gourhan argues that technics grow and evolve in parallel to humans as they evolve through the cummulative experiences between humans and tools. Since technology "escaped" us from the very beginning, it left our cerebral fields available to develop for everything else, such as abstract thought, art, and religion. This process is therefore not a threat but rather the "promise of an emancipation," a condition on which our very existence as Homo sapiens is based.

Overall, Leroi-Gourhan's paper is structured such that he first introduces this anxiety around technical overtaking and then bringing up his claim that this is far from a new problem. He then analyzes the prehistoric nature of humans and the growth of technology demonstrating his claim that this is by no means a new issue rather it is a key part of human history. Leroi-Gourhan then argues that technical overtaking has a "promise of an emancipation" which is what frees humanity from the need of an overdeveloped technical brain, allowing for more abstract thoughts.
