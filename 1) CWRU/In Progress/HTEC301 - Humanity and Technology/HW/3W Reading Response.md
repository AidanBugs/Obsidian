Wiener covered a wide variety of problems and concepts within this reading. The ones I found most interesting were relating the human machine relation to that of owner and slave in the past, the importance of game theory beyond its own field (wiener explicitly mentioned the military), as well as issues within these systems due to their often opaque nature. 

For starters, Wiener compares the human machine relation to that of master and slave, where the master wants a slave that is seemingly more capable than the master yet the slave is also compliently subservient to the master. Wiener argues of course this does not happen as a more intelligent slave is likely to defy the master arguing that complete intelligence and complete subservience are incompatible. Wiener argues that the smarter these machines get the more likely the are to overthrow us, yet he also argues that humans are many many levels above machines. Wiener broke down machines into 3 levels, lowest logical types which appear to be similar to an algorithm (following strict commands), then second logical type which improves its own algorithm overtime. Lastly he eludes to a notion of a higher level logical types (appearingly an iterative one without limits) which appears to be able to edit and improve upon its own learning method. Wiener argues that man reside in this higher level logical type and is unsure at what level the machines will surpass man, simultaneously still believing the human brain will be better than the constructed machine for a long time. 

Next I want to adress some of the applications of game theory and machine learning. For starters, Wiener mentions using machine learning and game theory to simulate games of "push-button war", which from my understanding is the idea that through automation we eventually get to some point where war is done solely through pushing buttons. I thought this was rather interesting, though Wiener notes that this would be unrealistic since the time it would take to train the machine would be long enough for the manners of which war is carried out is either so vastly different to what the machine was trained on OR would result in the machines winning us the war at an unforeseen cost. However beyond the ideas of a push-button war, in Wiener's "The Ontology of the Enemy", he discussed using machines to predict human pilot's flight paths and that use of machine learning in war. Now moving away from a pessimistic side of war, machine learning is also used in various medical fields to make predictions on patients diseases (although this has its own ethical issues). Most interesting to me are the applications of game theory. For starters, game theory is very commonly studied in the realm of business and economics due to applications on free market and prices of goods. However, game theory is also important in the realm of computer science, specifically in the realm of theoretical computer science, as it is used to theorize the limits of computation under given constraints. A commonly used idea is the idea of solving Totally Quantified Boolean Formula's (TQBF) which can be translated to a series of for every x1 there exists an x2 such that for every x3..... there exists an xn such that the statement is true. Which is commonly translated to moves in games, for every move the opponent makes, I can make a move where I will (eventually) win.
