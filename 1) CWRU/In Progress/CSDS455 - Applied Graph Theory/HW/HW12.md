# 1
If our bipartite graph is split into our vertex sets $V_1,V_2$ with only edges between vertices in $V_1$ to vertices in $V_2$. We can find a maximum bipartite matching for this graph by copying our bipartite graph with the same edges but making them directed from $V_1$ to $V_2$. Next we add a node $s$ which connects to all nodes in $V_1$ with each edge having a capacity of $1$. Subsequently we add an edge from every vertex in $V_2$ to a node $t$ with each edge here also having a capacity of $1$. Lastly we induce a flow by adding an edge with infinite capacity from $t$ to $s$ which has a cost of $-1$ and every other edge has a $0$ cost. 

All nodes start with $0$ supply and the optimal flow would be equal in magnitude to the size of the maximal matching (magnitude because our solution flow would have a negative cost).

Thus our optimal flow can be used to find the maximal matching by looking at which edges in $G'$ have flows and the edges that were in the original $G$ that have flows would be vertex disjoint and in our maximal match.

## Proof of correctness
Let $F(G')$ denote the cost optimal flow on our graph $G'$ described above and let $|M(G)|$ be the size of the maximum matching. Prove that $F(G')=|M(G)|$

Given our optimal matching $M(G)$, we know this is a vertex disjoint set of edges in $G$ and is the maximum size of any set of such vertex disjoint edges. Our optimal solution of $F(G')$ can use this set of edges in its optimal flows. Since the only negative edge (edge with any cost really) is from $t$ to $s$ we know that we must use this edge as much as we can. 

Suppose we take this edge with a flow of $|M(G)|$ (which is now our current $F(G')$), then $s$ currently has excess and $t$ has deficit of $|M(G)|$. Thus we split the supply in $s$ to the vertices that are part of $V_1$ and in our maximal matching. If $s$ still has supply left or is now in deficit then this creates a contradiction because it would imply that $|M(G)|$ is not equal to the number of $V_1$ vertices in the maximal matching, which is not possible because the number of vertices in a matching is $2$ times the number of edges in the matching, and since each edge has one vertex in $V_1$ and one in $V_2$ it should be split 50/50 so $|M(G)|$ in $V_1$ same amount in $V_2$. We know it is 50/50 because all edges are from $V_1$ to $V_2$.

We now have a set of vertices of size $|M(G)|$ in $V_1$ that have a $1$ supply, and we now flow from these nodes to nodes in $V_2$ along the edges in the maximal matching. So now all nodes in $V_1$ would have a supply of $0$, there is flow along the edges from $V_1$ to $V_2$ that correspond to the maximal matching and now nodes in $V_2$ have a combined supply of $|M(G)|$. This has to be the case following the similar logic of all edges being from $V_1$ to $V_2$ in the previous paragraph. Thus we take the edges from the nodes in $V_2$ to $t$ making every node have a supply of $0$ but now there is a flow where the total cost is $-|M(G)|$. But how do we know that two nodes in $V_1$ both had a flow to the same node in $V_2$ causing an arc from $V_2$ to $t$ to saturate at $1$ but leaving a node in $V_2$ with some supply. That would create a contradiction because it would imply that our matching is not vertex disjoint so that cannot happen.

How do we know there is not a more optimal flow that minimizes the cost further? Well that would imply there is some way to add a new flow from an unused vertex in $V_1$ to an unused vertex in $V_2$, which creates a contradiction because then this edge could be added to our match and still be vertex disjoint meaning we have a matching that is larger than our maximal matching.

Therefore $F(G')=|M(G)|$

# 2
Suppose we are trying to find the number of vertex disjoint paths from $u$ to $v$ in $G$, we can construct our $G'$ as follows. For any vertex $x$ in $G$ that is not $u$ or $v$, we essentially "split" it into $x_{in}$ and $x_{out}$. So all of f the incoming edges into $x$ point to $x_{in}$ and all outgoing edges from $x$ leave from $x_{out}$ and we create an arc between $x_{in}$ and $x_{out}$ with a capacity of $1$.

> Quick sidenote this works for undirected graphs as well because there are no symmetric arcs with this configuration!

$u$ and $v$ can keep the rest of their edges and do not need splittig and instead this can be our $s$ and $t$ where $u$ is $s$ and $v$ is $t$. Then we can either do a negative arc from $t$ to $s$ with infinite capacity and a cost of $-1$ to find our optimal solution giving us the magnitude of our optimal cost being the number of vertex disjoint paths OR we could have $s$ have a supply of $k$ and $t$ have a deficit of $k$ and iterate $k$ until there is no more feasible solution.

> We can also do this same $k$ strategy for $1$ but I like the negative edge better. 

## Proof of correctness. 
Well we know we use every vertex at most once because of the arc from $x_{in}$ to $x_{out}$ with a capacity of $1$. Meaning if two different paths used the same vertex it would mean that $x_{in}$ would end with a supply of $1$ and $x_{out}$ would have a supply of $-1$ which is not possible for a feasible flow. Thus we know that each path is vertex distinct in $G'$ but how do we know a MCNF finds an optimal solution.

Suppose the MCNF terminates at a $k$ smaller than the maximum number of vertex disjoint paths such that $k+1$ finds no feasible solution. Well our MCNF could have just taken the $|P|$ paths vertex disjoint paths from Mengers theorem and would have been able to take $|P|$ units of flow from $s$ to $t$ with only $1$ unit of flow per path (alligning with the $1$ flow capacity limits on the intermediate arcs). Since $k< |P|$ we could find a $k+1$ supply solution by using a subset of the paths showing that we are actually able to find a $k$ that is at least as large as $|P|$ 

Suppose the MCNF either terminates at a $k$ larger than the maximum number of vertex disjoint paths from Mengers theorem. This creates a contradiction because then our MCNF has found $k$ distinct/vertex disjoint ways/paths from $s$ ($u$) to $t$ ($v$). Thus we can find a new max paths set by taking all the paths with flows on them (with the contractions between any $x_{in}$ with the corresponding $x_{out}$). This creates a contradiction because we assumed we had a maximum vertex disjoint paths set. 

Therefore $k=|P|$ 

# 3 
Prove $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) \leq cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$ given that there are no edges from a node in $S$ to another node in $S$ (and same with $T$).

Since there are no edges within $S$ and $T$, the right side of our equation is the total number of edges that leave $S$ plus the total number of edges that leave $T$ ($cap$ does not care about incoming edges). The left side can only contain outgoing arcs from $S\cap T$ or $S\cup T$ implying that we only having outgoing arcs from $S$ and from $T$.

Suppose that $\exists S,T \subseteq V(G), s.t., cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) > cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$. This implies that the left side has more outgoing arcs then the outgoing arcs from $S$ and $T$ combined. Well we know that if $S$ and $T$ are disjoint then $cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T})$ is $0$ and $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T})$ is equal to the number of outgoing edges from $S$ and $T$ minus the ones that are from $S$ with a head in $T$ and vice versa. Thus $S$ and $T$ cannot be disjoint because then we have $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) \leq cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$. Suppose then $S=T$, then $S=S\cap T = S\cup T = T$ then we have $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S \cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) = cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$ which is not greater than so this does not work either.

Thus $\exists U = S\cap T$ and $S\neq T$. Since there are no edges between nodes within $S$ or $T$, then $cap(S, \overline S)= cap(U, \overline U) + cap(S-U, \overline{S-U})$ with the same logic for $T$. Thus we get the following simplifications:

> $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) > cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$

> $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(U, \overline{U}) > cap(S-U, \overline{S-U}) + cap(T-U, \overline{T-U}) + 2cap(U, \overline U)$

> $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) > cap(S-U, \overline{S-U}) + cap(T-U, \overline{T-U}) + cap(U, \overline U)$

This last statement cannot be true because even if $S$ and $T$ have no outgoing edges to eachother, it would imply that there are more outgoing edges in $S\cup T$ then its subparts of $T-U, U, S-T$. 

Therefore $cap(S\cup T, \overline{S\cup T}) + cap(S\cap T, \overline{S\cap T}) \leq cap(S, \overline S) + cap(T, \overline T)$
