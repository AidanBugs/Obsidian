# 1
From the definition of $k$-flow from Oct 12 we have the following:

1. for each directed edge $(x,y)$ then $f(x,y)=-f(y,x)$
2. for each vertex $v$, $\sum_{y\in N(v)} f(x,y)=0$
3. for each directed edge $(x,y)$, then $-k < f(x,y) < k$

Thus a valid $4$-flow is a $\forall e\in G, f(e)=0$. We can apply a similar principle to $Z_2\times Z_2$ where $\forall e\in G f_2(e)=(0,0)$.

Since $0$ flows are valid $4$ flows and $Z_2\times Z_2$ flows for any graph with edges, it follows that we have a valid $4$ flow iff we have a valid $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow.


# 2
## Proof for $\rightarrow$
Assume we have a nowhere-zero $4$-flow and show we have a $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow

Since in class we proved that we can change the direction of edges and still have a valid flow by flipping the sign of the flow. Thus we can deal with only the positive cases of $f_4(e)\in {1,2,3}$ and we need to now care (vaguely) about the direction of edges. As a rough start, for any edge $e$ with an odd $f_4$ flow, let $f_2(e)= (1,X)$ and any edge $e$ with even $f_4$ flow let $f_2(e)=(0,1)$. This is because the amount of odd flow incident edges to any vertex for the $f_4$ flows must be even so we can have $f_2(e)[0]$ to $1$ on only the odd edges to ensure that the sum for $a$ on a vertex to be even. 

For $e\in O, O$ being the set of odd edges in $f_4$, the $f_2(e)=(1,X)$ has an $X$ because there are cases where we would only have an odd amount of nodes with even flow in $f_4$. Thus we can define $X$ by chooding a cycle that uses only edges in $O$ and uses an odd one out even edge. Here an odd one out even edge would be any even edge that does not have a cycle of only even edges. So we say for $f_2(e)=(1,X)$ then $X=1$ iff $e$ was used in one of these cycles.

Thus we have a $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow because odd flows must from cycles and the even flows + these special odd edges must form cycles. 


## Proof for $\leftarrow$
Assume we have a nowhere-zero $Z_2\times Z_2$-flow and show we have a $4$-flow

From problem $3$ we have these sub graphs of even degree vertices. Thus for each subgraph we create cycles to produce a nowhere-zero $2$ flow by walking in cycles around the graph and if we traverse against a directed edge then we can assign that a flow of $-1$ otherwise leave as $+1$. Since every vertex is of even degree we know we can create cycles that span the edges of our graph. Thus we have a nowhere-zero $2$ flow for both subgraphs. I will label these flows as $g_1,g_2$ for the flows on $G_1,G_2$ respectively. We then can define $f_4(e)=g_1(e)+2g_2(e)$ and for any edge not in $G_1, g_1(e)=0$ similar idea for $g_2$.

We know that the resulting $f_4$ flow would be nowhere-zero because $g_1,g_2$ are nowhere-zero and have no way to sum together to be equal to $0$ thus $f_4\in \{\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3\}$. As for $f_4$ being a valid flow, in advanced algorithms we proved this idea of the sums of flows being a flow but I'll do a quick reproof here. If $g_1,g_2$ are valid flows then the sum of the flow at an individual vertex is $0$ and across any cut is also $0$ (by in class). Thus by combining these two flows that sum to $0$ at each vertex, satisfy $f(x,y)=-f(y,x)$ and across any cut is $0$ then we have a flow that satisfies all the definitions of flow.

Therefore $f_4$ is a valid nowhere-zero flow

## Proof for $\leftarrow$
Suppose we have $G=G_1\cup  G_2$ where all vertices in $G_1,G_2$ are of even degree. Thus we define our $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow on $G$ as $f(e)=(I_1,I_2)$ where $I_1=1$ iff $e\in G_1$ else $0$ and $I_2=1$ iff $e\in G_2$ else $0$. 

What this means is for any vertex $v\in G$, $\sum_{y\in N(v)} f(x,y)= (|N_1(v)| mod 2, |N_2(v)| mod 2)$ where $N_1(v)$ is neighbours of $v$ in $G_1$ (and similarly for $N_2(v)$ being for $G_2$). Thus our value is $(0,0)$ for a vertex iff the number of neighbours for $v$ is even for both $G_1,G_2$. Since we are given that $G_1,G_2$ are of even degrees for every vertex, this neighbour set for all vertices would be even in size. Therefore we have created a valid $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow for $G$ given that $G$ is the union of two all even degree graphs.

## Proof for $\rightarrow$
I'm not convinced this actually holds for edges with flow of $(0,0)$. A counter example being $K_2$ it has a valid $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow where its edge between the two vertices is flow $(0,0)$ but this can't be the union of two graphs of only even degrees.

Thus I am assuming that we have a nowhere-zero $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow on $G$. Similar to the previous direction, we say an edge is in $G_1$ iff $f(e)[0]=1$ and an edge is in $G_2$ iff $f(e)[1]=1$. We know that $\forall e\in G, f(e) \neq (0,0)$ thus each edge would either be in $G_1$ and/or in $G_2$. We also know that by the definition of $Z_2\times Z_2$ flow that the sum of flow along edges incident to a vertex should be even (so that way mod $2$ of the sum is $0$ for both $a,b$). This means that since $a$ mod $2=0$ then all vertices in $G_1$ would have an even amount of edges by our construction of $G_1$ and similarly since $b$ mod $2=0$ all vertices in $G_2$ are of even degree. We know that since we have a nowhere-zero flow that each edge would have some nonzero flow thus each edge in $G$ would either be in $G_1$ and/or $G_2$. Thus $G_1\cup G_2=G$ and $G_1,G_2$ are all even degree vertices.
