# Reading Response Paper 4
Mumford opens with a simple statement about how imrpovements in mathematics and sciences lead to a large revolution in technology. Mumford believes that if we continue on our current path, man will change from the active role of using tools to a much more passive slave to machines. Mumfords overall purpose for the paper is quite clearly stated, Mumford questions the powerful postion we have assigned to technology and to question the accepted theory of human nature that labels man as a mere tool maker. 

Mumford argues for centuries man has been defined as a tool-user where the nature of what makes us humans is the use of tools. He even states that "roughly shaped pebbles" near skulls is enough to define that skull as belonging to a human. However, Mumford points out the issue with this interpretation is that it neglects any form of organic materials and riches present as the only artifacts that would persist throughout these thousand of years are mere pebbles. As such, Mumford believes that it is foolish to let stone tools be the differentiator of humans and their more primal counterparts because there are other unpreserved indicators (such as more organic tools) and also the fact that the motor skills behind making these tools don't actually require much mental activity. 

Additionally, he argues that while tools are an important part of for humans (even in the early times of humans) it is important to not overextend their role as a tool rather than presuming their existence as an integral part of humanity. Mumford argues that other species have used tools, like birds with their nests, and thus technical skills alone are not a distinguishing factor for what a creature a human. As such, Mumford argues that the real distinguishing factor was having mental energy in excess of what is needed for survival, resulting in the creation of symbolic expressions and culture. 

Mumford states that human's greatest technological advancements were not driven by the need for food or survival, rather the desire to more fully express one's "superorganic needs". Arguing that the elaboration and development of symbolic culture vastly outpaced the external pressures of the environment. As such, he argues that rather than humans being defined as tool makers, he proposes a notion of humans being a "mind-using, self-mastering animal" where until a man has made something of himself he can make little of the world around him. Unfortunately for Mumford, the common notions in the 19th century were man as Homo Faber (tool maker) as opposed to Homo Sapiens (mind maker). 

However, Mumford believes strongly believes that it is more important to analyze how human's use these tools then give all the credit to the tools themselves. Mumford argues that trapping and foraging revolves more around the skill and sharp observations from the human rather than the ability of the tool. Furthermore, tools and weaponry were a small part of man's technical endeavors, rather advancements in culture and language were significantly more important part of human history as they were life-centered technologies. 

While there were many benefits to these life-centered technologies, they were challenged and, at times, replaced by these "Megamachines" which sought solely to increase the power and wealth of those already in power at the expense of a more rigid body of working labor. These "Megamachines" were simply large amounts of human's being forced to obey in order to achieve a singular goal. The rulers of such "Megamachines" had their power from some "cosmic source", which in many cases referred to some sort of power gained from religion. However, despite the clear social injustice within these machines, their results were undeniable which partially enabled such practices to continue. 

Mumford further argues that these "Megamachines" are still in place today with highly centralized powers that are now even more coercive, totalitarian, and irrational, stating these machines act as a limit for human potentiality. Modern notions of "absolute power" are akin to the bronze age's "Divine Kingship", stating that both logical frameworks act as dangerous delusions. As such, Mumford argues for a radical shift away from the "Megamachine" and return back to the age of more life-centered technics. 

To conclude, Mumford's paper is clearly lined out by first identifying the problems with notions of Homo Faber and suggests an alternattive more life-centered approach to what makes us humans. Mumford then addresses notions of the Megamachines and how they are actively dehumanizing us even at present times. As such, Mumford wishes to switch back to more life-centered technologies and dismantle the Megamachine.
